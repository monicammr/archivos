\snote{cost}

The dominant cost of the selection is the number of model simulations: the relative sensitivity matrix $J$ by central differences needs $2p$ simulations and the stage-2 ordering by finite effects $p$ more, $3p$ in total; all of them are independent and can run in parallel. The greedy selection, the conditioning tests, backward elimination and the first-order $e_{\mathrm{fit}}$ use only $J$ (linear algebra, negligible in comparison). The admissibility test adds, for each candidate subset, 15 scenario simulations and the re-estimations, whose cost depends on the number of retained parameters rather than on $p$. Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:cost} gives the measured time of one simulation of all measured outputs (all experimental conditions) at $\theta_0$ and the resulting time for the $3p$ simulations, in series and on four cores, for the problems timed.

\subsection*{Time of the complete method and comparison with profile likelihood}

The table above covers only the sensitivity computations. To measure the cost of the complete method, including the admissibility test (15 scenarios with re-estimation for every candidate subset) and backward elimination, we ran it from scratch, without any cached result, for all 34 problems, once with the sensitivity computations distributed over 16--64 cores and once on a single core per problem (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:tiempos}). The complete method took between 2~s and 33~min per problem (median 19~s) in parallel. For the six problems selected with the first-order $e_{\mathrm{fit}}$, these times do not include the nonlinear re-estimation of the final subset, which took from 4~min (Lucarelli) to 12.7~h (Chen) of processor time (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:timereview}). For Armistead, Lang and Raimundez the times were measured with a slightly different scenario set; the sensitivity simulations, which dominate the cost, are the same. Its cost is not proportional to $p$: it depends mostly on the simulation time and on the number of parameters re-estimated in the admissibility test, so that Smith (25 parameters, slow simulations, nine calibration parameters) took 19~min and Lang (294 parameters) about 3~min. Profile likelihood, with all the profile points of a problem computed simultaneously on 120 cores, was much slower whenever simulations are expensive (Alkan 27~min against 25~s; Isensee more than 1~h for the first round, and about 5~h including the re-estimations of the optimum (110~h of processor time), against 10~min; Zheng 5~min against 14~s), took 70~min for Bachmann and 11~min for Lucarelli, the two admissible problems with more than 60 free parameters in which it was run (on 60 cores; 10 and 35~s for our method), was not run for Chen and Raimundez, and was faster than our method on small problems, for which the fixed cost of the admissibility test dominates; over the 28 problems with both times our method was faster than the first round of profile likelihood in 12 (in 13 when all rounds are counted). All runs shared a 128-core server with other computations, so the times may be inflated by resource contention; main-text Fig.~4d plots the two times against each other.
