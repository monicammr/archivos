\snote{profile}

\subsection*{Purpose}
Profile likelihood (Raue et al., 2009) is the standard method to assess which parameters the data determine, and profile-likelihood-based reduction (Maiwald et al., 2016) uses it to decide which parameters to fix. We computed the profile of every biological parameter of every problem in which it was run, for two purposes: as an independent estimate of how many parameters the data determine, and as one of the four criteria with which our selection is compared (main text, \emph{Comparison with existing selection criteria}).

\subsection*{Design}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
\item The full model (all free parameters) was re-estimated on the training data of the external validation, from $\theta_0$, giving $\hat\theta_{\mathrm{train}}$ and the minimum $\chi^2_{\min}$. The finite-difference Jacobian of this fit was evaluated in parallel.
\item For each biological parameter $j$, $\log|\theta_j|$ was fixed at $\log|\hat\theta_{\mathrm{train},j}|\pm\{0.1,0.3,0.6,1.2,2.3\}$ (up to a factor of 10), within the same bounds $[|\theta_0|/10,10|\theta_0|]$ as all other fits, and all other free parameters were re-estimated from $\hat\theta_{\mathrm{train}}$; $\Delta\chi^2=\chi^2-\chi^2_{\min}$. All points of a problem were computed in parallel. Points whose fit failed (simulation failure, $\Delta\chi^2>10^4$) were excluded.
\item If a profile point improved the minimum by more than one unit of $\chi^2$, the full model had not converged: it was re-estimated from that point and all profiles were recomputed (at most three times; ten for Raia, which converged after four).
\item A parameter is \emph{identifiable} if its profile exceeds $\Delta\chi^2=3.84$ (95\%, one degree of freedom) on both sides, \emph{bounded on one side} if only on one side, and \emph{flat} otherwise. For the comparison, the $k=|S|$ parameters with the narrowest profile confidence interval were selected.
\end{enumerate}
The computation requires repeated re-estimation of the full model. It was run for the 26 problems with at most 60 free parameters and, in a separate run with the profile points distributed over 60 cores, for Bachmann (90 free parameters; 70~min, 40~h of computing time) and Lucarelli (72; 11~min, 9~h). It was not run for Raimundez (126) and Chen (155) because of its cost, nor for Blasi, which has no training/test split. Extrapolated from Bachmann with the simulation time and the numbers of free parameters and profile points, Raimundez (about 570 points, 0.3~s per simulation) would need of the order of $10^3$~h of computing time and Chen (about 1,520 points, 35~s per simulation) of the order of $10^5$~h.

\subsection*{Results}
Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:perfil} gives the classification of every problem. The median number of identifiable biological parameters was 2.5 (17\% of the biological parameters); six problems had none and 14 of the 28 had two or fewer. This agrees with the size of the subsets selected by our method, obtained by a different principle. The selected parameters, however, rarely coincide (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:selecciones}): a flat profile means that other free parameters can compensate for changes in the profiled parameter, whereas our method asks which parameters suffice when all others are fixed at $\theta_0$. Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:perfiles} shows the profiles of three problems. Three problems needed re-estimations of the optimum (Borghans, Okuonghae, Raia), which shows that the full-model fit from $\theta_0$ on the training data had not converged; Isensee kept a residual improvement of 1.16 after its three re-estimations, slightly above the criterion of one unit.

\emph{The two large problems.} Bachmann and Lucarelli were profiled in a separate run, with the same protocol and the profile points distributed over 60 cores. In Bachmann (90 free parameters, of which 27 biological and the rest calibration parameters), the 221 profile points took 70~min of wall-clock time and 40~h of computing time (median 5.4~min per point, at most 54~min), against 10~s for our complete method; 7 of the 27 biological parameters were identifiable, 5 bounded on one side and 15 flat. Profile likelihood selected \texttt{STAT5Imp} and \texttt{CISRNADelay}, which shares \texttt{CISRNADelay} with our subset (\texttt{CISRNADelay}, \texttt{JAK2ActEpo}); both subsets are admissible (median $e_{\mathrm{fit}}$ 0.30 against 0.34 for ours at $\pm$5\%, 0.30 at $\pm$20\%) and predict the real test data equally well (test $\chi^2/n$ 4.77 for both). In Lucarelli (72 free parameters, all biological), the 592 points took 11~min and 9~h of computing time (median 46~s per point), against 35~s for our method; 14 parameters were identifiable, 15 bounded on one side and 43 flat. The ten parameters selected by profile likelihood share four with our subset (\texttt{init\_Rec}, \texttt{S4tot}, \texttt{S\_phos}, \texttt{geneK\_turn}); they predict the real test data better (test $\chi^2/n$ 1.08 against 1.66) but do not reproduce the full model (median $e_{\mathrm{fit}}$ 0.74 at $\pm$5\% and 0.75 at $\pm$20\%, against 0.435 for our subset). Neither problem needed a re-estimation of the optimum. Profile likelihood was not run for Chen and Raimundez because of the cost given above.

\snote{morrisbrun}

\subsection*{Purpose}
The main text cites two further families of selection criteria: indices based on the collinearity of the sensitivity matrix, whose reference method is that of Brun \emph{et al.} (Brun et al., 2001), and global sensitivity screening, of which Morris's method (Morris, 1991) is the most widely used. We implemented both with the same protocol as the other criteria.

\subsection*{Design}
\emph{Brun.} Dimensionless sensitivities $s_{ij}=\theta_j\,\partial y_i/\partial\theta_j$ on the training outputs (forward differences with relative step $10^{-4}$), importance $\delta^{\mathrm{msqr}}_j=\|s_{:,j}\|/\sqrt n$, collinearity index $\gamma_K=1/\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\tilde S_K^\top\tilde S_K)}$ of the column-normalised subset and determinant measure $\rho_K=\det(S_K^\top S_K)^{1/(2k)}$. Among the 15 most important parameters, every $k$-subset was evaluated and the one with $\gamma_K\le10$ and the largest $\rho_K$ was selected (the one with the smallest $\gamma_K$ if none satisfies the bound). The importance $\delta^{\mathrm{msqr}}_j$ orders the parameters as the energy $E_j$ of our method; the difference lies in the selection rule and in the absence of a re-estimation test.

\emph{Morris.} Ten trajectories (seed 42) on a four-level grid of $\log|\theta_j|$ within $[\theta_0/10,10\theta_0]$ for the biological parameters (calibration parameters at $\theta_0$), step $\Delta=2/3$ of the range; elementary effect $\mathrm{EE}_j=\|y(x+\Delta e_j)-y(x)\|/\Delta$ on the training outputs; the $k$ parameters with the largest $\mu^\ast_j=\mathrm{mean}|\mathrm{EE}_j|$ were selected.

Chen was not included, because the three selections (pivoted QR, Brun and Morris) on its training outputs require 1,734 simulations of 35~s each (about 17~h of computing time) before any re-estimation. Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:selecciones} lists the parameters selected by all five criteria.

\subsection*{Implementation differences between the criteria}
All criteria chose the same number $k$ of biological parameters and were evaluated with the same tests, but they were not implemented with identical numerical settings. The differences, which a strictly controlled comparison would remove, are:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item \emph{Data used to select.} Our method selects on all measured outputs of the problem; pivoted QR, Brun, Morris and profile likelihood select on the training outputs of the external validation. This is why Bertozzi, whose selected parameters act only in the test conditions, was excluded from the admissibility comparison (main text). In the other problems the training set is a subset of the outputs used by our method.
\item \emph{Derivatives.} Our method uses central differences in $\log\theta$ with relative step $10^{-2}$; pivoted QR and Brun use forward differences with relative step $10^{-4}$; Morris uses finite elementary effects of $2/3$ of the logarithmic range (a factor of about 21 in the parameter), so it measures global rather than local sensitivity, whereas the admissibility test is local ($\pm$5\% and $\pm$20\%).
\item \emph{Candidates.} Brun evaluates all $k$-subsets of the 15 most important parameters only; the other criteria consider all biological parameters. Columns with zero sensitivity are discarded by pivoted QR and Brun (norm below $10^{-10}$) and by our method (zero energy).
\item \emph{Normalisation and ties in pivoted QR.} The columns are normalised to unit length, as is standard, so the magnitude of the sensitivity is discarded. As a consequence, all columns have the same norm at the first pivoting step, and the first parameter is chosen among them by rounding differences and column order; only the subsequent pivots are determined by the residual norm, that is, by collinearity with the parameters already chosen. This is a property of normalised pivoted QR, not of our implementation, and it contributes to its low admissibility.
\item \emph{Computational budget.} The criteria do not use the same number of simulations: pivoted QR and Brun need $p+1$ simulations, Morris $10(p+1)$, profile likelihood ten re-estimations of the full model per parameter, and our method $3p$ simulations for $J$ and the stage-2 ordering plus the re-estimations of the admissibility test for every candidate subset.
\end{itemize}
These differences do not by themselves invalidate the comparison, and some of them disadvantage our method (for example, profile likelihood uses far more computation), but the comparison should be read as a comparison of the criteria as usually implemented, not of criteria that differ only in their selection rule. We did not repeat the comparison with harmonised settings. The profile-likelihood classification has two further caveats, marked in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:perfil}: in Borghans and Isensee a profile point still improved the optimum by more than one unit of $\chi^2$ after the last re-estimation (1.56 and 1.16), so their full-model optimum did not converge and their classification is provisional; and failed profile points (simulation failure or $\Delta\chi^2>10^4$) were excluded, which can make a parameter look less identifiable than it is (three parameters in Okuonghae and Weber).

\snote{admisother}

\subsection*{Purpose}
Our method returns a subset only if re-estimating it reproduces the full model under random parameter changes (admissibility). This note applies exactly the same test to the subsets of the other criteria, to determine whether they have this property without being selected for it.

\subsection*{Design}
The test replicates the code of the method: 15 scenarios $\theta^{(k)}=\theta_0\odot(1+\varepsilon u^{(k)})$ with the same seed (42), re-estimation of the subset together with the calibration parameters from two starting points, scenarios with non-finite outputs discarded, and the subset is admissible if the smaller of the median $e_{\mathrm{fit}}$ and the median $e_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is at most $\tau_e=0.436$ (with $|S|\ge2$), exactly as for our method (Methods of the main text; with nonlinear re-estimation the median $e_{\mathrm{fit}}$ is never larger than the median $e_{\mathrm{rel}}$, so the rule reduces to the median $e_{\mathrm{fit}}$). The eight problems that the method treats to first order were treated in the same way for every criterion. As a check, the test reproduces the published $e_{\mathrm{fit}}$ of our subsets (for example Armistead 0.330, Boehm 0.094, Fiedler 0.147). Because our subsets are selected by this test at $\varepsilon=0.05$, it was repeated at $\varepsilon=0.20$, a level used by none of the criteria.

\subsection*{Results}
Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:admis} and Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:admismapa} give the result of every criterion in every problem, and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:mcnemar} the paired comparison. At $\pm$5\% our subsets were admissible in all 28 problems, against 26 (Brun), 23 (Morris), 18 of 27 (profile likelihood) and 14 (pivoted QR); at $\pm$20\%, 27 of 28 against 25, 23, 18 of 27 and 15. Bertozzi is not included: our method selected its parameters on all measured outputs, which in this problem include the test conditions, whereas the other criteria selected on the training outputs, so the criteria did not use the same information. There is no problem in which the subset of another criterion is admissible and ours is not. The only problem in which our subset loses admissibility at $\pm$20\% is Elowitz, one of the problems that are admissible with a small margin at $\pm$5\% (main text, \emph{Robustness}).

\snote{compsynth}

\subsection*{Design}
The synthetic prediction experiment (\sref{synth}) was repeated with the subsets of every criterion, with the same 20 seeds (42--61), so that the five criteria are evaluated on exactly the same truths and noise realisations. Each subset, with the calibration parameters, was re-estimated on the synthetic training data from $\theta_0$ and $e_{\mathrm{pred}}$ was computed on the test data. The comparison uses the cases common to the five criteria in which the parameter change exceeded the noise (282 cases in 24 problems). Because the 20 seeds of a problem are not independent, the main statistic resamples whole problems (Methods, \emph{Statistics}).

\subsection*{Results}
Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:synthmethods} gives the results per problem and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:synthstats} the statistics. Our subsets reached $e_{\mathrm{pred}}\le\tau_e$ in 56\% of the cases, those of Brun in 55\%, Morris 48\%, profile likelihood 39\% and pivoted QR 34\%; the advantage of our method over pivoted QR (22 percentage points, 95\% confidence interval 10--34), profile likelihood (17, 8--28) and Morris (9, 2--16) is significant, whereas the difference from Brun is not. The per-problem median prediction error did not differ significantly between criteria (Wilcoxon $p\ge0.07$). Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:synthsemillas} shows that the ordering of the criteria is similar across seeds.
